# Power, Language and Urban Sustainability
## Toward a Performative Planning Theory of Urban Policy

*Book proposal and innovation rationale for Springer Nature (preferred series: The Urban Book Series). Draft of 25 September 2026. The evidence base comes from a Consensus search of the peer-reviewed literature.*

---

## 1. The innovation in one paragraph

Cities publish more sustainability plans than ever, yet what the plans *say* and what cities *become* keep drifting apart. Planning theory has well-developed accounts of **power** (communicative, Foucauldian, agonistic and post-political) and of **language** (argumentative, discursive and narrative turns). It has no integrated account of **how power decides whether sustainability language actually does anything**. This book fills that gap with a **Performative Planning Theory**. It treats plans, visions and sustainability commitments as *speech acts* that succeed or misfire depending on four power-laden **felicity conditions**: **Authority, Articulation, Translation and Materialisation**. The book turns the theory into a practical method, the **Sustainability Speech-Act Audit**, and a **Performativity Index**. It also extends planning theory into two areas it has barely addressed: **linguistic plurality and epistemic justice**, and **generative AI and large language models (LLMs) as new speakers in planning**.

## 2. Five gaps in planning theory that the book addresses

### Gap 1: Power is theorised in rival camps, with no bridge between them
- Critics argue that communicative planning theory assumes power can be temporarily suspended to reach consensus [2]. Foucauldian scholars counter that planning theory must study "what is actually done" rather than "what should be done" [4], [5].
- A 40-year review of Forester, Healey and Innes finds that communicative planning says more about power than its critics admit. It still leaves **conceptual voids regarding constitutive *power to* and legitimate *power over*** [3].
- Recent work calls for **an analytical framework that bridges normative theorising and the empirical configurations of power** in urban governance [1]. Others call for embracing, rather than dichotomising, the tensions between the camps [6].
- The post-political critique, in which sustainability consensus evacuates "the political" [9], [10], [11], is itself criticised as a **"post-political trap"**. That critique reduces urban politics to a binary and cannot see in-between, contingent forms of agency [12], [13].

**What is missing:** a theory of power that works at the level of *utterances and texts*. It should explain why some statements carry force and others do not, without choosing between Habermas, Foucault and Mouffe.

### Gap 2: Language is studied in three parallel traditions that rarely meet
- Discourse analysis (hegemony), framing analysis (meaning construction) and narrative analysis (storytelling) share foundations but are **usually applied separately**. A 2024 review had to build a heuristic simply to combine them [15].
- The narrative turn in planning (Throgmorton, Sandercock, Ameel) shows that planning "is performed through story" [17], [18], [19]. It has not produced an account of **how stories become binding policy and built form**.
- Discourse analysis is a powerful tool for urban policy research, but it carries methodological pitfalls and is **rarely linked to outcomes** [16].
- Organisation studies has moved further than planning. It treats strategy texts as having **"force potential"** through self-authorisation, special terminology, forced consensus and deonticity [20]. It treats texts as agents [37] and theorises the **talk–action gap** of aspirational commitments [39]. Planning has only scattered uses of performativity [40], [41], [42].

**What is missing:** a unified theory of *how language acts* in planning, where discourse, frame and narrative become one analytical chain.

### Gap 3: "Sustainability" has great discursive power but weak material force
- Sustainability works as a **vehicular idea**, "a linguistic political mechanism with no essence other than the capability to attract positive affections" for coalitions. It stays hegemonic even when outcomes are decoupled from environmental and social goals [22].
- Transformation-oriented vocabularies can become **"ivy discourses"**, radical in wording yet reproducing the mainstream [23]. Liveability discourse conceals residents' lived experience behind rankings [24]. Social sustainability can be **"hijacked"** as it is translated between municipal departments [25]. Sustainability can also serve as a **"sustainability fix"** that lets growth proceed [26].
- The proliferation of city labels (sustainable, smart, resilient, eco, low-carbon and more) produces conceptual vagueness [27]. That vagueness matters politically, because cities are now framed as the solution rather than the problem [28].
- The same word carries **different ideologies at different development scales** within one planning system [29].
- The implementation gap is large and documented: an "insufficient materialisation of sustainability objectives" [30], a land-use target implementation rate of only **15.91%** sustained by "growth-first" discursive practices [31], and Olympic sustainability promises that fell far short in translation [32], [33].

**What is missing:** a theory explaining **when sustainability words perform and when they are non-performatives**, that is, speech acts that "do not bring about the effects that they name" [38].

### Gap 4: Planning theory is monolingual and Northern
- Planning theory has long been shaped by the Global North. It is unsettled by the "clash of rationalities" in Southern cities [45] and by insurgent planning [46]. **Pluriversal** scholarship is now emerging [47].
- **Language justice is described as an under-examined part of the sustainability knowledge–action gap**, given the dominance of English in sustainability [43]. The concepts of **linguistic injustice and epistemicide** have entered the sustainable development literature [44].
- Epistemic injustice in planning now has an analytical framework, illustrated with an informal settlement in Iran [48]. Storytelling is being decolonised [49]. Critical discourse analysis combined with ecolinguistics shows how Indonesia's new capital uses sustainability language to legitimise epistemic erasure [50].
- Studies of how sustainability "travels" (policy mobility, SDG localisation) treat translation as institutional adaptation [26], [34], [35]. They rarely treat it as **translation between languages**, where meanings are gained and lost.

**What is missing:** a theory that treats **translation across languages, scales and knowledge systems** as a site of power in urban sustainability.

### Gap 5: Machines have become planning "speakers", and theory has not noticed
- LLM agents now **simulate thousands of residents** in participatory land-use planning [53] and create **synthetic inhabitants and experts** [55]. They also **summarise and classify public feedback** in real plan changes [54].
- "City brains" use LLMs to **generate visions of urban futures** that feed policy, which risks a loss of transparency, accountability and human stakeholders [56].
- A review of civic participation in AI finds that most authors **do not challenge expert authority or the status quo** [57]. Data itself operates as a discursive practice that depoliticises urban sustainability [58].

**What is missing:** planning theories of language assume human speakers. None asks **who holds authority, voice and accountability when a machine drafts the sustainability plan or speaks for "the public"**.

## 3. Core contribution: the Felicity Framework for Urban Sustainability

J. L. Austin showed that a performative utterance ("I promise", "I declare") only works if certain *felicity conditions* hold. Otherwise it "misfires". The book applies this idea to urban sustainability: **a plan's sustainability commitments are performatives whose felicity is set by power.**

| Felicity condition | Guiding question | Power dimension | Linguistic markers analysed | Typical misfire |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Authority** | Who is recognised as entitled to speak sustainability, and who is not? | Power *over* and legitimacy; "recognised speech" [14]; epistemic injustice [48] | Speaker positions, self-authorisation, who is quoted or silenced | Experts and developers speak; residents are "consulted" but not heard |
| **2. Articulation** | How is sustainability worded, framed and narrated? | Discursive and hegemonic power [4], [21] | Lexicon and city labels, metaphors, storylines, nominalisation, agentless passives, modality ("shall" vs. "should / aim to / encourage") | Vague, consensual "vehicular" wording with no binding force [22] |
| **3. Translation** | What happens to meaning as it moves across scales, sectors, languages and algorithms? | Relational and network power; policy mobility [26], [32] | Intertextuality, boundary objects, translation shifts between languages, LLM summaries | Justice and ecological limits drop out while growth survives [25], [29] |
| **4. Materialisation** | Do words become law, budgets, infrastructure and measurable change? | Power *to*: institutional and material capacity [8], [42] | Named agents, deadlines, budgets, indicators, legal instruments | Non-performative: the commitment names an effect it never produces [38] |
| **Feedback: Contestation** | Who can answer back, and does counter-speech shift authority? | Counter-power, agonism, insurgency [7], [46] | Counter-narratives, media framing, protest language | Dissent is "choreographed" and absorbed [10] |

### Five propositions the book develops and tests
1. **Authority proposition:** sustainability language performs only when spoken from positions that control resources. Where authority is dispersed (partnerships, "soft spaces"), sustainability tends to become non-performative.
2. **Coalition–force trade-off:** the vaguer and more consensual a sustainability signifier, the wider the coalition it can assemble, and the weaker its illocutionary force.
3. **Selective translation:** each translation (across scale, sector, language or algorithm) re-weights meaning in favour of dominant interests. Social-justice and ecological-limit meanings are the most likely to be lost.
4. **Materialisation:** commitments with named agents, deadlines, budgets and binding modality are measurably more likely to be implemented. The Performativity Index is designed to test this.
5. **Re-politicisation:** counter-speech restores felicity when it changes *who is authorised to speak*, not only *what is said*.

## 4. Methodological innovation: the Sustainability Speech-Act Audit (SSAA)

The SSAA is a replicable protocol, supplied as electronic supplementary material with a codebook and templates. It combines critical discourse analysis, narrative analysis, corpus linguistics and discourse network analysis:

1. **Corpus building.** Assemble plans, strategies, council minutes, consultation reports, media and, where used, AI-generated summaries, in their original languages.
2. **Commitment extraction.** Identify every sustainability "commitment utterance".
3. **Felicity coding.** Code each commitment for:
   - speaker and authority
   - explicit or deleted agent
   - modality (deontic or hedged)
   - time-bound (yes/no)
   - resourced (yes/no)
   - indicator (yes/no)
   - legal instrument (yes/no)
   - frame and storyline
   - translation path
4. **Performativity Index.** Calculate the share of commitments that meet the felicity conditions, at the plan level and by sustainability dimension (environmental, social, economic).
5. **Outcome tracing.** Compare the index with implementation evidence (budgets, permits, built projects, indicators) to measure the **sustainability performativity gap**.
6. **Counter-speech mapping.** Map oppositional narratives and whether they shift authority over time.

This moves discourse analysis from *interpreting* texts to *explaining outcomes*, which answers the critique that discourse research is disconnected from material results [16], [30].

## 5. Chapter-by-chapter synopsis

### Part I – Foundations: Words, Power and the City

**1. Introduction: Why Urban Sustainability Is a Problem of Power and Language**
Opens with the paradox of cities that "say" sustainability ever more loudly while missing their targets. Introduces the sustainability performativity gap, the book's research questions and the argument that power decides whether sustainability language performs. Sets out the structure, methods and intended readers.

**2. Power in Planning Theory: From Rational Control to Communicative, Foucauldian and Agonistic Readings**
Traces how planning theory has conceptualised power, from synoptic rationality through Habermasian communicative planning, Foucauldian analytics, agonism and the post-political critique. Uses typologies of *power over / with / to* and of reinforcive, innovative and transformative power [3], [8]. Diagnoses the missing bridge between normative and empirical accounts [1].

**3. Language in Planning Theory: The Communicative, Argumentative, Discursive and Narrative Turns**
Reviews how planning has understood language: as deliberation (Forester, Healey), argument (Fischer and Forester), discourse and storyline (Hajer, Richardson) and story (Throgmorton, Sandercock, Ameel). Shows that the three analytical traditions of discourse, frame and narrative remain parallel [15]. Argues that none of them explains how words become worlds.

**4. How Plans Do Things with Words: Speech Acts, Performativity and the Sustainability Performativity Gap**
The theoretical core. Draws on Austin, Searle and Butler, on textual agency [37], on the force of strategy texts [20] and on non-performatives [38] to recast plans as speech acts. Introduces felicity conditions and misfires, and defines the sustainability performativity gap. Presents the Felicity Framework and five propositions.

### Part II – The Felicity Framework for Urban Sustainability

**5. Authority: Who Is Entitled to Speak Sustainability? Expertise, Legitimacy and Recognised Speech**
Examines how expert, political, corporate and community voices gain or lose the authority to define sustainability. Draws on "recognised speech" and moderated consensus [14], on epistemic injustice [48] and on critiques of communicative planning [2]. Shows how authority is produced, not given.

**6. Articulation: The Grammar of Sustainable Cities – Labels, Frames, Storylines and Modality**
Analyses the lexicon of city labels [27], vehicular and ivy discourses [22], [23], liveability [24] and resilience framings. Introduces a grammar-level toolkit (nominalisation, agent deletion, modality, metaphor, presupposition) for reading plans. Develops the coalition–force trade-off.

**7. Translation: How Sustainability Travels Across Scales, Sectors and Languages**
Brings policy-mobility research [26], [32], [33], SDG localisation [35] and boundary-object research [25] into planning theory. Adds the neglected dimension of translation between languages and translanguaging [43]. Shows how meanings are selectively lost or gained on their journey from global agendas to local plans.

**8. Materialisation: From Policy Words to Urban Worlds**
Follows sustainability words into zoning codes, budgets, permits, infrastructure and indicators, drawing on actor-network theory [42], research on implementation gaps [30], [31] and discursive lock-ins [36]. Explains why some commitments become concrete while others remain non-performatives. Links materialisation back to power *to*.

### Part III – Contested and Emerging Arenas

**9. Counter-Speech: Insurgent Narratives, Agonism and the Re-politicisation of Sustainability**
Analyses how residents, movements and the media answer back through counter-narratives [51], insurgent practice [46], agonistic media arenas and populist rhetoric [52]. Moves beyond the "post-political trap" [12], [13]. Identifies the conditions under which counter-speech changes who is authorised to speak.

**10. Plural Languages, Plural Sustainabilities: Southern and Decolonial Perspectives on Planning Discourse**
Centres Southern theory [45], pluriversal scholarship [47] and decolonial storytelling [49]. Examines linguistic injustice and epistemicide [44] and cases where sustainability language legitimises erasure [50]. Proposes language justice as a planning principle.

**11. Algorithmic Speakers: Generative AI, Large Language Models and the Automation of Planning Discourse**
Examines LLM-simulated residents [53], [55], AI summaries of public comments [54] and generative city brains [56]. Applies the Felicity Framework to machine-generated planning language. Asks who is the speaker, who is accountable and whose language the model reproduces, and proposes governance principles for AI in participatory planning [57].

### Part IV – Methods and Practice

**12. The Sustainability Speech-Act Audit: A Method for Critical Planning Linguistics**
Presents the SSAA protocol step by step, with codebook, coding examples, the Performativity Index and guidance on reliability. Explains how to combine critical discourse analysis, narrative analysis, corpus tools and discourse network analysis, and how to use AI tools reflexively in the analysis itself. Templates are provided as electronic supplementary material.

**13. Applying the Framework: Comparative Case Studies of Urban Sustainability Plans**
Applies the SSAA to three or four contrasting cities, selected from the author's fieldwork: for example, a celebrated European "green" city, a Global South megaproject or new capital, a Middle Eastern metropolis and an East Asian smart city. Compares each city's Performativity Index with its implementation outcomes. Tests the five propositions.

**14. Conclusion: Toward a Performative Planning Theory for Just Urban Sustainability**
Synthesises the theoretical contribution and revisits the propositions. Sets out implications for planners (writing commitments that perform), educators (teaching critical planning literacy) and policymakers (governing AI and multilingual participation). Closes with a research agenda.
